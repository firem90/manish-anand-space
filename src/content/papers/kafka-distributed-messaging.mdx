This paper is deceptively short, but it captures the core insight that made Kafka the backbone of modern data infrastructure: **treat every data flow as a log.**

## The Problem It Solved

LinkedIn's data pipeline was a mess of point-to-point connections. Every new consumer of data required a new integration. The system didn't scale organizationally, let alone technically.

## The Key Design Decisions

**Pull-based consumption** is the single most important decision in Kafka's design. By letting consumers control their own offset into the log, Kafka eliminates the need for complex broker-side state management. A consumer can replay, rewind, or skip ahead independently.

**Partitioned, append-only logs** give Kafka its throughput characteristics. Writes are sequential I/O (fast on spinning disks, absurdly fast on SSDs). Reads are sequential too, since consumers process partitions in order.

**No per-message acknowledgment** — Kafka tracks offsets, not individual messages. This is a dramatic simplification compared to traditional message queues like RabbitMQ. The tradeoff is that you might reprocess messages after a consumer crash, which means your consumers must be idempotent.

## What Changed My Thinking

Before reading this paper, I thought of message queues as infrastructure for "sending messages between services." After reading it, I understood them as **distributed commit logs** — an abstraction that unifies messaging, event sourcing, CDC, and stream processing.

The paper convinced me that the log is the foundational data structure for distributed systems. If you get the log right, everything else — databases, caches, search indexes — are just materialized views of that log.
